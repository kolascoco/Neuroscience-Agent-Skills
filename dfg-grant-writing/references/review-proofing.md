# Review-Proofing a DFG Proposal

How proposals fail in review, and how to write so they do not. Use when drafting sections
1–2, and again as a final pass before submission.

---

## 1. How reviewers actually read

Reviewers are competent scientists, usually not specialists in the exact sub-field, working
through several proposals. They read the summary, then skim for the objectives and the work
programme, then return to section 1 to judge whether the gap is real. They are **not
required to read any cited work**.

Three consequences:

- The argument must be legible on a single pass. Bury the key claim and it will be missed.
- Anything load-bearing must be *in* the text, not in a reference.
- Vagueness reads as absence. "We will investigate X" tells a reviewer nothing about whether
  the applicant knows how.

---

## 2. The objections that sink proposals

| Objection | Prevention |
|-----------|-----------|
| "The gap is asserted, not demonstrated." | Show what has been done and where it stops; cite the closest prior work and say precisely what it leaves open. |
| "This is incremental." | Name what becomes possible that was not possible before. |
| "Feasibility is unproven." | Preliminary data, established methods in the group, or a work package that de-risks the rest first. |
| "The work programme is under-specified." | Per WP: goal, rationale, methods, outcome, timeline. Numbers, not adjectives. |
| "Sample size is unjustified." | A priori power analysis, or — for adaptive/pilot designs — simulated operating characteristics. Say which and why. |
| "The design cannot test the hypothesis." | State, for each hypothesis, the contrast that tests it and the control that isolates it. |
| "The budget does not follow from the work." | Trace every item to a work package (see `modules-and-budget.md`). |
| "Overambitious for the duration." | Give a timeline with dependencies, and name what happens if a stage runs late. |
| "The clinical claim is overstated." | Keep the mechanism claim and the translational aspiration clearly separated. |

---

## 3. Pre-empt objections inside the text

An objection the reviewer raises is a weakness; the same objection raised and answered by
the applicant is evidence of command of the field. When a design choice has an obvious
alternative, name the alternative and say why it was not chosen.

> "A sham arm is not included. The question is not whether stimulation has an effect —
> that is established — but whether the timing manipulation reduces the dose required. The
> comparison that answers this holds site, dose and task constant and varies only timing;
> a sham arm would differ in every respect at once and could not."

Two cautions. Keep pre-emption **short** — a paragraph, not a page — and put it where the
choice is made, not in a separate defensive section. And do not pre-empt objections nobody
would raise; it draws attention to weaknesses that were not there.

---

## 4. Framing limitations

Limitations belong in the proposal. Their absence reads as naivety, but the framing decides
whether they cost anything.

**Structure a limitation in four moves:** name it plainly → say why it is a genuine risk →
give the reasons it is nonetheless the right choice → state the escalation path if it turns
out badly. The fourth move is what converts a weakness into evidence of planning.

**Prefer a single named risk to a scattering of hedges.** Where several weaknesses share one
cause, say so — three separate weaknesses invite three separate objections, whereas one
underlying limitation admits one answer.

**Do not conceal a risk the reviewer will find.** If a design accepts a known cost for
feasibility, say that explicitly. "This risk is accepted in exchange for feasibility, and
here is how it is bounded" is far stronger than silence.

---

## 5. Critiquing prior work

Section 1 usually has to establish that an existing approach is insufficient. The trap is
sounding dismissive of a literature the reviewer may have contributed to.

- **Establish the approach as valid first**, then locate the limitation. A method that is
  "validated, and precisely because it is validated its inefficiency is now measurable"
  reads as a refinement; the same point made against an unproven method reads as an attack.
- **Quantify the limitation** where possible. A cited dose, response rate or effect size is
  an argument; "suffers from variability" is an opinion.
- **Make the limitation the one your project fixes.** If the critique and the proposal do
  not line up exactly, one of them is wrong.
- **Never imply prior work was careless.** The limitation should follow from the design of
  the method, not from the competence of its authors.

---

## 6. Pilots, feasibility studies and small samples

Small studies are fundable when framed as what they are.

- A pilot's primary outcome is **feasibility** — recruitment, retention, tolerability,
  completion. Effect sizes are **estimation targets** for powering a later trial, not
  hypothesis tests.
- Say explicitly that the study is **not powered for confirmatory inference**. A pilot that
  promises to demonstrate efficacy fails if it does not; a pilot that promises to estimate
  an effect and establish feasibility succeeds either way.
- Where a larger effect is expected in a clinical population, present it as a quantity to
  estimate and name the countervailing factors, rather than assuming the favourable outcome.

---

## 7. Hypotheses and design logic

- Each hypothesis: **directional, singular, falsifiable**, mapped to a work package and an
  outcome measure.
- Prefer a **predicted dissociation** to a general effect — a crossover interaction is more
  falsifiable, and more interesting, than "both conditions will help".
- Do not hypothesise **equality** ("the effect will be equally large at both sites") unless
  it is genuinely predicted; a null there is otherwise scored as a failed prediction.
- Where hypotheses depend on each other, state the chain, so the set reads as designed
  rather than assembled.
- Distinguish **planned** comparisons from exploratory ones, and say which were pre-specified.

---

## 8. Between-study and cross-study comparisons

If a claim rests on comparing results from two sub-studies, say so explicitly, describe what
is matched (task, endpoint, dose, schedule) and what is not (usually participants), and
pre-specify the comparison in the analysis plan. A comparison a reviewer discovers is a
confound; a comparison the applicant declares and justifies is a design feature.

---

## 9. Final pass questions

Read the draft once through asking only these:

1. Can a non-specialist state the gap after reading section 1 once?
2. Does every hypothesis have a work package, an outcome measure and a control?
3. Could a competent person execute the work programme from this description?
4. Is every number in the budget traceable to a sentence in the work programme?
5. Is any load-bearing claim resting on a citation rather than on the text?
6. Are risks named, bounded and given a fallback?
7. Does anything overclaim — clinically, causally, or about what the data can show?
8. Are 2.4 (research data) and 2.5 (sex/gender/diversity) both substantive?
9. Is own work distinguishable from others' work on every page?
10. Would the applicant be comfortable if the closest competitor reviewed this?
