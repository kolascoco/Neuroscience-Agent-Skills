# DFG Proposal Language and Style Guide

## Fundamental Principle

A DFG proposal is a persuasion document, not a lecture. Every sentence serves one of two purposes:
1. Convincing reviewers the research question matters
2. Convincing reviewers *you* can answer it

If a sentence does neither, cut it.

---

## Language Choice: English vs. German

**Choose English** if:
- English is the primary publication language of your field
- International reviewers are likely (DFG actively seeks broad reviewer pools)
- You write more precisely in English

**Choose German** if:
- Your field primarily publishes in German (some humanities, law, certain social sciences)
- German is required or strongly conventional for the specific call

**Never mix languages** within the project description. Pick one and stay consistent, including in headings, tables, figure captions, and the bibliography.

The project summary in Part A must be provided in *both* German and English regardless of which language is used for the project description.

---

## Register and Tone

DFG proposals occupy a precise register: **formal scientific prose, confident but not overclaiming**.

### Target the right reader

Write for an informed scientist *outside your sub-field* — someone who understands research methods and can evaluate feasibility, but may not know your specific literature. If a term needs two sentences to explain, it probably needs a brief definition. If it needs a paragraph, reconsider whether it belongs in a proposal at all.

The project summary (Part A) must be readable by a *general* audience — drop all jargon here.

### Confidence calibration

| Too uncertain | Right level | Overclaiming |
|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| "It might perhaps be possible to…" | "We hypothesise that X, which we will test by…" | "We will definitively show that…" |
| "Some researchers have suggested…" | "Evidence from [Smith 2022] suggests…" | "It is established that…" (if it isn't) |
| "We hope to obtain results…" | "We anticipate [specific outcome] based on [preliminary data]" | "We will obtain [specific outcome]" |

Use hedged language for predictions ("we anticipate", "we expect", "we hypothesise") but direct language for what you will *do* ("we will measure", "we will analyse", "we will develop").

## Sentence-Level Style

### Active voice
Write actions with clear agents. Active voice signals ownership, accountability, and forward momentum.

- **Avoid:** "The samples will be analysed by mass spectrometry."
- **Prefer:** "We will analyse samples by mass spectrometry."
- **Avoid:** "It has been shown that…"
- **Prefer:** "Smith and Jones (2021) showed that…" — name the agents

### Sentence length and structure
- Average sentence: 20–25 words. Occasional short sentences (10 words) for emphasis are good.
- Avoid stacking subordinate clauses. Each subordinate clause halves reader attention.
- One idea per sentence at the core; qualifications in subordinate position, not leading.

**Weak:** "Despite the fact that previous studies, which were conducted using older methodologies that did not account for confounding factors, have suggested a correlation, the relationship remains unclear."

**Strong:** "Previous studies suggested a correlation, but methodological limitations — particularly uncontrolled confounds — leave the relationship unresolved. Our study addresses this directly by [method]."

### Paragraph structure
- First sentence = topic sentence (the claim of the paragraph)
- Middle sentences = evidence, elaboration, or justification
- Last sentence (optional) = transition or implication

If you cannot write a one-sentence summary of a paragraph, it probably contains two ideas.

### Noun strings and nominalisations
German academic style tends to pack information into noun compounds. In English (and in accessible German), prefer verb-based constructions.

- **Avoid:** "The implementation of data collection procedures for the measurement of…"
- **Prefer:** "To collect data on…" or "We will measure…"

## Section-Specific Language Patterns

### Section 1 — State of the Art

**Gap story structure:**
1. Establish what is known (broad context, 2–3 sentences)
2. Narrow to the specific problem (the gap)
3. Articulate why the gap matters (significance)
4. Position your project as the solution

Opening sentence: do not begin with "In recent years…" or "It is well known that…" — these are filler. Start with the substantive claim.

**Example gap sentence:** "Despite extensive characterisation of X, the mechanism by which Y triggers Z remains unknown — a gap that prevents development of [application]."

Cite specific literature to justify each claim. "Some studies suggest" is vague; "Smith (2020) and Lee (2022) both found" is precise.

### Work Programme

Use numbered work packages (WPs). Each WP should follow this pattern:
1. **Goal** — one sentence stating what this WP achieves
2. **Rationale** — why this WP is necessary and in this order
3. **Methods** — specific enough to evaluate feasibility, not a methods textbook chapter
4. **Expected output** — what will exist at the end (data, model, publication, software?)
5. **Timeline** — months required

Avoid vague verbs: "investigate", "explore", "study", "look at" — replace with: "measure", "quantify", "test [specific hypothesis]", "develop [specific model]", "compare [X] and [Y] using [method]".

**Weak:** "In WP3 we will investigate the role of protein X in disease Y."
**Strong:** "WP3 tests whether protein X activates pathway Z in disease Y, using CRISPR-mediated knockout in [cell line] combined with transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq). This directly addresses hypothesis H2. Expected output: identification of ≥3 downstream targets of X for validation in WP4."

### Objectives Section

State objectives as concrete, deliverable outcomes — not aspirations. Use parallel structure.

**Weak:**
- "To better understand mechanism X"
- "To gain insights into the role of Y"

**Strong:**
- "To determine whether [X] regulates [Y] under [conditions Z]"
- "To develop a predictive model of [phenomenon] validated against [dataset]"
- "To identify [specific entity] responsible for [specific function]"

### Budget Justification

Write one justification paragraph per budget category. Be specific.

**Weak:** "We request funding for one postdoctoral researcher for 24 months."

**Strong:** "We request a postdoctoral position (E13, 100%, 24 months) to conduct WP2 (synthesis and characterisation, months 7–30). This requires advanced expertise in [technique], which the PI's group does not currently include. The 24-month timeline reflects the planned synthesis cycles (4 × 5 months, plus 4 months for data analysis and manuscript preparation)."

For equipment: demonstrate the project-specific need and confirm it is not part of standard departmental infrastructure.

For travel: name the events or institutions and state the scientific purpose (collaborator visit, conference presentation, field work).

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Content mistakes
- **Recycled project:** Proposing to fund work already completed. Even if unpublished, reviewers notice if preliminary data look suspiciously complete.
- **Underselling novelty:** Failing to articulate what is genuinely new vs. incremental.
- **Overselling scope:** A single 3-year Individual Research Grant cannot transform a field. Size the ambition to match the budget and timeline.
- **Weak work programme:** Listing activities without explaining how they address the stated hypotheses.
- **Missing risk discussion:** Every project has risks. Acknowledging them and proposing mitigations signals scientific maturity.
- **Omitting RDM:** Research data management is mandatory and belongs in **section 2.4** of the current (06/26) form. Reviewers will flag its absence.

### Language mistakes
- Starting the project summary with jargon
- Using passive voice throughout (signals diffidence; obscures responsibility)
- Long noun strings instead of verb-based sentences
- Mixing German and English
- "et al." in the bibliography (DFG requires all author names)
- Using "recently" or "currently" without a date anchor (these become outdated)
- Citing unpublished, non-public work as if it were available literature

### Formatting mistakes
- Using a font smaller than Arial 11pt (instant flag for reviewers)
- Exceeding page limits in sections 1–3
- Using an outdated form version
- Missing sections (especially 2.5 sex/gender/diversity and 2.4 research data)
- PDF with security restrictions preventing copying

## Checklist: Is This Sentence Working?

Before finalising any paragraph, ask:

1. Does this sentence tell the reviewer something they need to know to evaluate the proposal?
2. Is the agent clear? Who does what?
3. Could this be said in fewer words without losing precision?
4. Is the claim calibrated? (Not too certain, not too hedged)
5. Does it follow naturally from the sentence before it?

If the answer to any of 1–5 is "no", revise before moving on.
